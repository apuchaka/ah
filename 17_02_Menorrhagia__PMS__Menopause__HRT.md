## Menorrhagia

**D**: heavy menstrual bleeding that interferes with quality of life.

**Causes**
- Dysfunctional uterine bleeding (menorrhagia without underlying pathology)
- Anovulatory cycles
- Uterine fibroids
- Hypothyroidism
- Intrauterine devices (copper coil)
- Pelvic inflammatory disease
- Bleeding disorders

**Ix**
- FBC, haematinics if anaemic, TSH, cervical smear, STI screen (as indicated by history)
- If risk factors for endometrial cancer or history suggestive of endometrial pathology: refer for outpatient hysteroscopy ± biopsy
- Imaging: US

**Mx**
- **Mirena IUS**: ↓bleeding via intrauterine levonorgestrel release (causes endometrial atrophy); SE: irregular bleeding for first 4–6 months + progestogenic effects
- **COCP**: effective in ↓bleeding but many contraindications
- **3rd line**: IM progestogens (e.g. medroxyprogesterone)
- If patient does not require contraception: mefenamic acid (useful if dysmenorrhoea also present) or tranexamic acid — start on first day of period
- **Surgery**: indicated for fibroids >3cm, inadequate response to previous treatment, or declined medical Mx — endometrial ablation, myomectomy, hysterectomy

## Premenstrual syndrome (PMS)

**D**: repetitive, cyclical, physical and behavioural symptoms occurring in the luteal phase of the normal menstrual cycle.

**A/P**: serotonin and GABA implicated.

**S/smx**: anxiety, stress, fatigue, mood swings, bloating, breast pain.

**Mx**
- **Mild**: lifestyle changes — regular sleep, exercise, smoking cessation, ↓alcohol; regular, frequent, small, balanced meals rich in complex carbohydrates
- **Moderate**: new-generation COCP (e.g. Yasmin)
- **Severe**: SSRI — can be taken continuously or only during luteal phase (e.g. days 15–28 of cycle)

### Premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD)
Most severe form of PMS.

> [!info] DSM-5 criteria
> **≥1 of the following must be present:**
> - Mood swings, sudden sadness
> - Anger, irritability
> - Sense of hopelessness, depressed mood, self-critical thoughts
> - Tension, anxiety, feeling on edge
>
> **Plus ≥5 total symptoms**, including:
> - Difficulty concentrating
> - Change in appetite
> - Diminished interest in usual activities
> - Easy fatiguability, ↓energy
> - Feeling overwhelmed
> - Breast tenderness (cyclical mastalgia), bloating, weight gain, joint/muscle aches
> - Sleeping too much or not enough

## Menopause

**D**: period of waning fertility leading up to the last period.

> [!info] Verified against the Australasian Menopause Society (AMS), Aug 2026 — Australian average menopause age is **51 years**, essentially the same as the UK figure (52yo) — not a meaningful difference, but stated as the AMS's own figure rather than a UK statistic. AMS specifically notes most women become menopausal between 50–54 years, with <40 classified as premature menopause and 41–45 as early menopause — terminology worth using consistently with the rest of this entry.
> Average age in Australia is 51yo (AMS).

- Retrospective diagnosis when menses has ceased for ≥12 consecutive months, not attributable to other reasons
- **Premature menopause**: <40yo
- **Perimenopause**: period marked by menstrual irregularity, ending 12 months after final menstrual period

**R**: 40–60yo (tends to occur earlier — in 40s — in Asian women), cancer treatment, smoking (2y earlier), ovarian surgery.

**A**: natural process triggered by declining oocyte numbers.

**P**: ↓ovarian production of progesterone, oestradiol, and testosterone → various symptoms.

**S/smx**
- Vasomotor (80%): hot flushes (may occur every few minutes for >10y), night sweats, palpitations
- Urogenital changes (35%): vaginal dryness/atrophy, urinary frequency, breast/skin atrophy
- Psychological: 10% have anxiety and depression ± short-term memory impairment
- Long-term complications: ↑cardiovascular disease risk, ↑osteoporosis risk

**Mx**
- Confirm menopause (rule out thyroid and psychiatric problems)
- Diet and exercise changes
- Menorrhagia: Mirena coil; consider referral for hysteroscopy if symptoms suggest endometrial pathology
- Use contraception until >1y amenorrhoea if >50yo, >2y if <50yo
- Vaginal dryness: oestrogen cream 0.1% PV nightly for 2w, then 2x/week as required
- HRT (see below)

## Hormone replacement therapy (HRT)

**D**: oestrogen + progesterone replacement.

**Indications**
- Menopausal symptoms: vasomotor, mood disorders, urogenital symptoms
- Premature menopause: primarily for osteoporosis prevention — continue until 50yo

**Benefits**
- ↓vasomotor symptoms via oestrogen — benefit by ~4w, max effect at 3 months
- Urogenital symptoms — benefit may take several months; continue long-term
- ↓osteoporotic fracture risk — sustained/lifelong treatment necessary
- ↓colorectal cancer risk

**Risks**

> [!info] Verified against the Australasian Menopause Society (AMS), Aug 2026 — **the figures below are genuinely consistent with current AMS risk communication**, not UK-specific numbers needing replacement; AMS independently cites the same ~2.3% per-year-of-use breast cancer risk figure and describes it as "very small" in absolute terms, similar in magnitude to other common risk factors like being overweight. AMS-specific framing worth adding: **no increased cardiovascular risk if MHT (AMS's preferred term — Menopausal Hormone Therapy, used interchangeably with HRT) is started within 10 years of menopause onset or before age 60** — the "timing hypothesis," a genuinely important nuance the original note doesn't capture; VTE risk with patches/gels is minimal-to-none, while tablets roughly double the (still very low) baseline risk — consistent with the "lower risk with patches" note below.
> - Breast cancer risk ↑ by 2.3% per year of use, dependent on duration and type of HRT; risk ↓ to baseline 5y after stopping. Highest risk with combined HRT (not seen in those starting HRT for premature menopause); different progestogens carry different risk levels — micronised progesterone and dydrogesterone appear to carry lower risk than synthetic progestogens such as norethisterone
> - Endometrial cancer (RR 2.3) if unopposed oestrogen used in those with a uterus; risk persists ≥5y after stopping
> - ↑VTE risk (≥2x) with oral tablets, though absolute risk remains small (~1 extra case per 1,000 women); minimal or no increased VTE risk with patches or gels; most likely in the first year of use

**Contraindications**
- Breast cancer (past or present)
- Oestrogen-dependent cancer
- Undiagnosed vaginal bleeding
- Untreated endometrial hyperplasia
- Previous idiopathic or current VTE (unless already anticoagulated)
- Active or recent arterial thromboembolic disease (e.g. MI)
- Active liver disease with abnormal LFTs
- Pregnancy
- Thrombophilic disorder
