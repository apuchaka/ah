## Acute Confusion

- **D:** an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness developing over hours to days — **delirium**. The two features that separate it from dementia are **acute onset** and **impaired attention**, and the fluctuation means a single lucid assessment does not exclude it. **Hypoactive delirium — the quiet, withdrawn, drowsy patient — is commoner than hyperactive delirium and is missed far more often**, which is the single most useful thing an intern can know here.
- **DDx — the causes are systemic far more often than neurological.** A structured mnemonic beats free recall at 3am:
  - **Infection:** urinary tract infection, pneumonia, cellulitis, intra-abdominal sepsis, **meningitis and encephalitis**, COVID and influenza.
  - **Drugs — the commonest single reversible group:** **opioids, benzodiazepines, anticholinergics** (oxybutynin, amitriptyline, promethazine), antipsychotics, corticosteroids, digoxin, lithium, levodopa, antiepileptics; **and withdrawal — alcohol, benzodiazepines, nicotine**. Review the chart for what was started, what was stopped, and what was missed.
  - **Metabolic:** **hypoglycaemia** (checked immediately, in everyone), hyponatraemia and hypernatraemia, hypercalcaemia, uraemia, hepatic encephalopathy, thyroid disease, **hypoxia and hypercapnia**, thiamine deficiency (**Wernicke's encephalopathy**).
  - **Structural/neurological:** stroke, intracranial haemorrhage, **subdural haematoma — particularly in an older person on anticoagulation after a fall that may have seemed trivial**, tumour, **non-convulsive status epilepticus** (consider in unexplained persistent confusion), post-ictal state.
  - **Cardiorespiratory:** myocardial infarction (may present as confusion alone in older people), heart failure, pulmonary embolism, arrhythmia, shock of any cause.
  - **Environmental and iatrogenic:** **urinary retention and constipation** (genuinely common and genuinely reversible), pain, **sleep deprivation and ward environment**, catheters and lines, recent surgery and anaesthesia, dehydration, sensory impairment with missing glasses or hearing aids.
- **Focused Hx:** **a collateral history is the investigation, not an optional extra** — from family, carer, GP or residential care staff. Establish **the patient's baseline cognition and function**, the **time course** of the change, and **fluctuation**. Then: complete medication reconciliation including recent changes, ceased drugs and over-the-counter agents; alcohol and other substance use with the last drink; falls and head injury; fever and localising infective symptoms; bowel and bladder function; pain; sleep; previous delirium episodes; sensory aids.
- **Examination:** **vital signs including temperature, oxygen saturation and capillary glucose** — the last is not optional. Then: conscious level and **a formal attention test** rather than a general impression; full neurological examination looking for focal signs, neck stiffness, papilloedema; **hydration status**; **palpate for a distended bladder and examine for faecal loading — both are frequently the whole answer**; full infective screen including skin, chest, abdomen and lines; signs of chronic liver disease; **asterixis**; signs of alcohol withdrawal; evidence of head injury; medication patches (fentanyl patches are missed on examination routinely).
- **First-line Ix:** **capillary glucose immediately**, then **FBC, CRP, UEC, calcium and magnesium, LFTs, TFTs, glucose**; **urinalysis and urine culture** — with the important caveat that **asymptomatic bacteriuria is very common in older people and a positive urine dipstick alone does not explain delirium**; treating it and stopping the search is a recognised error; **blood cultures if febrile**; **chest X-ray**; **ECG**; **arterial or venous blood gas** where hypoxia or hypercapnia is plausible; **B12, folate and thiamine** where nutrition is poor; **CT brain** where there are focal signs, head injury, anticoagulation, or no cause is found after a systematic search; **lumbar puncture** where meningitis or encephalitis is suspected; **EEG** where non-convulsive status is a genuine consideration.

> [!info] **Use a validated screening tool rather than an impression.** Australian hospitals commonly use a short delirium screen at admission and with any change in behaviour. **The specific tool and its cut-off score are deliberately not stated here** — Australian tools and thresholds could not be confirmed against a primary source under the egress limitation, and quoting a cut-off from memory is precisely the failure mode this project documents. Use whichever screen your hospital mandates, and score it properly rather than eyeballing it.

## Reduced Consciousness

- **D:** impaired arousal, ranging from drowsiness to unresponsiveness. **This is an airway problem until proven otherwise** — the first action is not diagnosis but assessment of whether the patient can protect their airway.
- **DDx:**
  - **Immediately reversible — check these first, in this order:** **hypoglycaemia**; **hypoxia**; **hypercapnia**; **opioid toxicity** (pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression); **benzodiazepine toxicity**; **shock of any cause**.
  - **Structural intracranial:** stroke (particularly brainstem and large hemispheric with mass effect), intracerebral haemorrhage, **subarachnoid haemorrhage**, subdural or extradural haematoma, tumour with oedema, hydrocephalus, abscess.
  - **Metabolic and systemic:** hyponatraemia, hypernatraemia, hypercalcaemia, uraemia, **hepatic encephalopathy**, DKA and HHS, hypothyroidism/myxoedema coma, adrenal crisis, hypothermia and hyperthermia, **Wernicke's encephalopathy**.
  - **Infective:** meningitis, encephalitis, cerebral malaria in a returned traveller, sepsis with encephalopathy.
  - **Toxicological:** alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, tricyclics, antipsychotics, gamma-hydroxybutyrate, carbon monoxide, deliberate self-poisoning with any agent.
  - **Seizure-related:** post-ictal state, **non-convulsive status epilepticus**.
- **Focused Hx:** almost always collateral. **What was the patient doing and how were they found**; time last seen well; **access to medications and to alcohol or other drugs**, including what is in the house; suicide note or empty packets; head injury; recent illness, fever or headache; known epilepsy, diabetes, liver, kidney or cardiac disease; anticoagulation; recent travel; and **the ambulance record and observations en route**, which frequently contain the diagnosis.
- **Examination:**
  - **A–E first.** Airway patency and protection, breathing pattern and rate, oxygenation, circulation, **capillary glucose**, temperature.
  - **Quantify the conscious level with a validated scale and document the components**, not just the total — the breakdown is what shows deterioration.
  - **Pupils: size, symmetry and reaction** — **pinpoint suggests opioids or a pontine lesion; a unilateral fixed dilated pupil suggests uncal herniation and is an emergency**; both are actionable at the bedside.
  - **Focal neurology**, tone, reflexes, plantar responses, and **posturing**.
  - **Neck stiffness** (with cervical spine precautions if trauma is possible), **fundoscopy for papilloedema**, signs of head injury including **scalp haematoma, CSF otorrhoea or rhinorrhoea, Battle's sign and periorbital bruising**.
  - **Full skin survey** — needle marks, **medication patches**, rash, purpura.
  - Signs of chronic liver disease, asterixis, ketones on the breath.
- **First-line Ix:** **capillary glucose and an arterial or venous blood gas immediately** (the gas gives pH, CO₂, lactate, sodium, potassium and haemoglobin within minutes); **FBC, UEC, calcium, magnesium, phosphate, LFTs, coagulation profile, CRP, TFTs, cortisol** where adrenal crisis is possible; **paracetamol and salicylate levels, ethanol, and a targeted drug screen** in any suspected overdose; **blood cultures**; **ECG**; **chest X-ray**; **CT brain — early and with a low threshold**, particularly with focal signs, head injury, anticoagulation or an unexplained picture; **lumbar puncture after CT** where CNS infection is suspected, **without delaying antibiotics**; **carboxyhaemoglobin** where carbon monoxide is possible; **β-hCG**.

> [!danger] **Give thiamine before glucose in any patient at risk of thiamine deficiency** — alcohol dependence, malnutrition, hyperemesis, bariatric surgery. A glucose load in a thiamine-deplete patient can precipitate Wernicke's encephalopathy. **The dose and route are deliberately not stated here**, being egress-blocked Australian guideline content; use the local protocol, but know that the *sequence* matters.

## Acute Focal Neurological Deficit

- **D:** sudden loss of a discrete neurological function — weakness, sensory loss, speech disturbance, visual loss, ataxia. **The working diagnosis is stroke until proven otherwise, and the entire assessment is built around time**, because the treatments are time-limited.
- **DDx:**
  - **Vascular:** **ischaemic stroke**, **intracerebral haemorrhage** (clinically indistinguishable from ischaemic stroke at the bedside — **only imaging separates them**, which is why no antithrombotic is given before the scan), **transient ischaemic attack**, subarachnoid haemorrhage, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, arterial dissection (consider in a younger patient, especially with neck pain or recent trauma).
  - **Stroke mimics — around one in five suspected strokes, and worth knowing because they change management:** **hypoglycaemia** (the most important, and excluded in seconds), **seizure with Todd's paresis**, **migraine with aura**, **functional neurological disorder**, sepsis or metabolic derangement unmasking a previous deficit (**recrudescence of an old stroke**), space-occupying lesion, subdural haematoma, Bell's palsy, peripheral nerve lesion, multiple sclerosis relapse, Wernicke's encephalopathy.
- **Focused Hx:** **the time the patient was last known well — established precisely, from a named person, and documented.** This single datum determines eligibility for reperfusion therapy, and "found down on waking" is not the same as onset time. Then: exact deficit and whether it is progressing, improving or fluctuating; **seizure at onset**; headache and its character; neck pain; trauma; **anticoagulants and antiplatelets with the time of the last dose**; recent surgery or bleeding; previous stroke or TIA; atrial fibrillation; hypertension, diabetes, smoking, dyslipidaemia; pregnancy; **baseline function and independence**, which informs the treatment discussion; and **advance care directives** where relevant.
- **Examination:** **capillary glucose first** — it is a stroke mimic that is fully reversible and takes seconds to exclude. Then vital signs including **blood pressure in both arms** and temperature; **a structured stroke severity assessment** covering conscious level, gaze, visual fields, facial and limb power, sensation, language, dysarthria, neglect and ataxia; **swallow screen before anything is given orally, including medications** — a genuinely intern-level responsibility with direct aspiration consequences; cardiovascular examination for **atrial fibrillation, murmurs and carotid bruits**; fundoscopy; and signs of trauma.
- **First-line Ix:** **immediate non-contrast CT brain** — the single most urgent investigation, to distinguish haemorrhage from ischaemia; **CT angiography** (and perfusion imaging where the local pathway uses it) to identify large vessel occlusion; **capillary glucose**; **FBC, UEC, coagulation profile including INR, LFTs, glucose, lipids, HbA1c**; **ECG** for atrial fibrillation, with prolonged monitoring if the initial trace is sinus; **troponin**; **β-hCG**; **echocardiography** and **carotid imaging** as part of the aetiological work-up.

> [!danger] **The time windows, stated because they drive the whole assessment.** **Intravenous thrombolysis is recommended within 4.5 hours of symptom onset** (Australian Stroke Foundation guidelines and international guidance agree on this figure). **Endovascular thrombectomy** is available for selected patients with large vessel occlusion, and **advanced imaging has extended selection out to 24 hours in appropriately selected cases** — selection is by imaging criteria and specialist assessment, not by the clock alone.
> **Specific thrombolytic agents, doses, and blood pressure thresholds for treatment are deliberately omitted** — they are protocol-specific, they differ between Australian services, and the Stroke Foundation guideline was egress-blocked. The intern-level actions are: **recognise, protect the airway and swallow, check the glucose, establish the time last known well, escalate immediately, and do not give aspirin or any antithrombotic before the CT.**

## Thunderclap Headache

- **D:** a headache of **explosive onset reaching maximum intensity within seconds to about a minute**. The defining feature is **the speed of onset, not the severity** — a patient describing "the worst headache of my life" that built over an hour is not a thunderclap headache, and one that peaked instantly but is now moderate still is. This distinction is the entry's main teaching point, because it is what determines whether the SAH pathway is triggered.
- **DDx:**
  - **Subarachnoid haemorrhage — the diagnosis the presentation exists to exclude**, and around one in ten to one in eight thunderclap headaches. Aneurysmal in most cases.
  - **Other vascular:** **cervical or intracranial arterial dissection**, **cerebral venous sinus thrombosis** (consider particularly in pregnancy, postpartum, on the oral contraceptive pill, or with thrombophilia), **reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS)** — recurrent thunderclap headaches, often triggered by exertion, sexual activity or vasoactive drugs, **posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES)**, pituitary apoplexy, hypertensive emergency, intracerebral haemorrhage.
  - **Non-vascular:** **bacterial meningitis** (can present abruptly), **spontaneous intracranial hypotension** (a headache that is much worse upright and relieved lying flat — a positional headache with a thunderclap onset), third ventricle colloid cyst, acute obstructive hydrocephalus, **acute angle-closure glaucoma** (headache with a red painful eye, blurred vision and haloes — an ophthalmic emergency that presents to medical teams), sinusitis with acute obstruction, cough or exertional headache (a diagnosis of exclusion), **primary thunderclap headache** — also strictly a diagnosis of exclusion.
- **Focused Hx:** **time to peak intensity, asked explicitly and in seconds** ("how long from the first twinge to the worst it got?"); what the patient was doing at onset (**exertion, straining, sexual activity, coughing**); **previous identical episodes** (RCVS is recurrent; a first SAH is not); associated **neck stiffness, photophobia, vomiting, seizure, transient or persistent loss of consciousness, focal deficit, visual change or diplopia**; **positional variation — worse upright suggests intracranial hypotension**; fever; pregnancy and postpartum status; hormonal contraception; **vasoactive drugs — cocaine, amphetamines, cannabis, decongestants, triptans, SSRIs** (RCVS); anticoagulation; **family history of subarachnoid haemorrhage or polycystic kidney disease**; smoking, hypertension and alcohol; recent head or neck trauma or manipulation; previous headache pattern and how this one differs.
- **Examination:** **vital signs including blood pressure and temperature**; **conscious level, documented formally**; **neck stiffness and Kernig's/Brudzinski's signs**; **full cranial nerve examination — a third nerve palsy with a dilated pupil suggests a posterior communicating artery aneurysm and is a critical finding**; **fundoscopy for papilloedema and subhyaloid/retinal haemorrhage**; **pupils, visual acuity and assessment for a red, hard, painful eye** (angle-closure glaucoma); focal neurological signs including limb power, sensation, coordination and plantar responses; signs of trauma; **assessment of whether the headache changes with posture**.
- **First-line Ix:**
  - **Urgent non-contrast CT brain** — first-line, and **its sensitivity for subarachnoid blood is highest immediately after onset and falls progressively with time from onset**, which is why the delay from headache onset to scan must be recorded and communicated. *(A specific sensitivity percentage for the early-scan window is deliberately omitted: the figure was not confirmable to three independent sources under the egress limitation, and it is a number that drives a decision to withhold a lumbar puncture — precisely the kind of figure that must not be reproduced from memory.)*
  - **Lumbar puncture if the CT is non-diagnostic and suspicion persists** — for **red cell count in sequential tubes and, critically, xanthochromia**, which remains the reference standard for SAH with a negative CT. **Xanthochromia requires time to develop, so the LP is conventionally delayed to at least around 12 hours from headache onset**; check opening pressure at the same time, since a low pressure supports intracranial hypotension and a high one supports venous sinus thrombosis or hydrocephalus.
  - **CT or MR angiography** where dissection, RCVS or venous sinus thrombosis is suspected — **CT venography specifically for cerebral venous sinus thrombosis**, which a plain CT will not exclude.
  - **FBC, UEC, LFTs, CRP, coagulation profile, glucose, β-hCG**; **blood cultures and empirical antibiotics without delay** if meningitis is suspected.
  - **Intraocular pressure measurement** if angle-closure glaucoma is possible.

> [!warning] **A normal CT does not exclude subarachnoid haemorrhage**, and this is the error that causes preventable death in this presentation. If the history is genuinely thunderclap, the pathway continues — lumbar puncture or angiography per local protocol — regardless of a reassuring scan, and discharging on the strength of a normal CT alone is not safe practice.
