## Rhinosinusitis

- **D:** inflammation in the nose and paranasal sinuses with ≥2 symptoms: nasal blockage/obstruction/congestion; nasal discharge; facial pain/pressure; reduction or loss of smell; endoscopic or CT signs of sinus or middle meatus obstruction.
- **Chronic rhinosinusitis:** >12 weeks of rhinosinusitis.

### Acute rhinosinusitis

- **Predisposing factors:** atopy (hay fever, asthma); nasal obstruction (e.g. septal deviation or nasal polyps); recent local infection (e.g. rhinitis or dental extraction); swimming/diving; smoking.
- **S/Smx:** facial pain — typically frontal pressure pain, worse on bending forward. Nasal discharge — usually clear if allergic or vasomotor; thicker, purulent discharge suggests secondary infection. Nasal obstruction (e.g. "mouth breathing"). Post-nasal drip — may produce chronic cough (DDx: GORD).

> [!warning] Severe rhinosinusitis criteria — ≥3 of: discoloured discharge, severe local pain, fever, ↑ESR/CRP, "double sickening" (deterioration after an initial milder phase).

- **DDx:** migraine, TMJ dysfunction, dental pain, neuropathic pain, temporal arteritis, herpes zoster.

### Management (by severity)

- **Mild** (≈ common cold): self-limiting. Analgesia, nasal saline irrigation, decongestants. If symptoms >10 days, trial intranasal corticosteroids (e.g. mometasone). If symptoms persist >14 days further, refer to ENT.
- **Moderate:** intranasal steroid spray. If symptoms >14 days, refer to ENT.
- **Severe:** topical steroids & antibiotics. Reassess at 48h — if improvement, continue treatment for 14 days. If no improvement, refer to ENT.
- General advice: avoid allergens; intranasal corticosteroids; nasal saline irrigation. If indicated by culture, a long-term antibiotic course (e.g. 12 weeks of a macrolide) may be needed.

> [!danger] Red flags: unilateral symptoms; persistent symptoms despite 3 months of treatment; epistaxis; numbness.

- **Ix:** endoscopy to look for nasal polyps, culture, imaging.

### Rhinosinusitis with polyps

- **Children:** rule out neoplasms, cystic fibrosis, meningocele/encephalocele.
- **Mild disease in adults:** intranasal steroids + review at 3 months.
- **Moderate:** trial steroid spray ± doxycycline for 20 days; review at 1 month.
- **Severe:** topical steroids ± short course of PO steroids; review at 1 month. If no improvement, CT scan ± surgery.

### Complications of sinusitis

- Orbital cellulitis/abscess.
- Intracranial involvement: meningitis, encephalitis, cerebral abscess, cavernous sinus thrombosis.
- Mucoceles.
- Osteomyelitis.
- Pott's puffy tumour (subperiosteal abscess arising from frontal osteomyelitis).

### Cancer of the paranasal sinuses

- Suspect when chronic sinusitis presents for the first time in later life.
- **S/Smx:** blood-stained nasal discharge, nasal obstruction.
- **Ix:** MRI/CT ± endoscopy & biopsy.
- **Types:** SCC (50%), lymphoma (10%), adenocarcinoma, etc.
- **Mx:** radiotherapy ± radical surgery.

## Nasal fractures

- **Dx:** new nasal deformity, associated with facial swelling, black eyes.

> [!danger] Rule out head or C-spine injury. Look for a septal haematoma — boggy swelling of the septum causing near-total nasal obstruction; if present, requires urgent incision and drainage.

- **Mx:** treat epistaxis; analgesia, ice; close skin injury. Reassess 5–7 days post-injury once swelling has resolved. If manipulation under anaesthesia is required, do this 10–14 days after injury (before the nasal bones are set).

## CSF rhinorrhoea

- **A:** ethmoid fractures disrupting the dura and arachnoid; also consider tumour.

> [!info] Gap-filled — the classic bedside signs of basal skull fracture (the injury underlying most traumatic CSF rhinorrhoea) were never mentioned despite their direct relevance here. **Battle's sign:** bruising over the mastoid process, from blood tracking posteriorly along the temporalis fascia from a **middle cranial fossa** fracture — classically appears **1–3 days after injury**, not immediately, given the time needed for blood to track and become visible subcutaneously; its delayed onset is a genuinely important point, since a normal mastoid examination immediately post-injury doesn't exclude this fracture. **Racoon eyes (periorbital ecchymosis):** bilateral bruising around the eyes from blood tracking anteriorly into the periorbital soft tissue from an **anterior cranial fossa** fracture — the specific fracture location these two signs each localise to is the testable distinction, not just "bruising after head injury." Both signs, alongside CSF rhinorrhoea/otorrhoea and haemotympanum, form the classic basal skull fracture examination findings.

- **Dx/Ix:** CSF sampling — positive for glucose, β2-transferrin.
- **Mx:** if traumatic, conservative management; usually resolves spontaneously. 7–10 days bedrest with head elevated 15–30° ± lumbar drain. Avoid coughing, sneezing, nose-blowing. Cover with antibiotics and pneumococcal vaccine.

## Epistaxis

- **D:** bleeding from the nostril, nasal cavity, and/or nasopharynx; may be classified as anterior or posterior. Acute or chronic (>12 weeks).
- **R:** dry weather, nasal trauma, primary coagulopathy (and other conditions predisposing to bleeding, including cancer).
- **A:** 95% of bleeding originates in Little's area, located in the anterior inferior septum (Kiesselbach plexus).

> [!note] **Recurrent, unexplained, or persistently unilateral epistaxis** — particularly alongside other unilateral symptoms (nasal obstruction, discharge, hearing loss) — should prompt consideration of nasopharyngeal cancer, see below, not repeated here; this is genuinely different from the typical bilateral, self-limiting, dry-weather-related epistaxis most presentations represent, and shouldn't be treated with the standard first-aid approach alone if these features are present.

### Management

- ABCDE, resuscitate if needed.
- History: which side, trauma, quantify blood loss, on warfarin or aspirin? PMH.
- Ask the patient to apply pressure by pinching the lower part of the nose for 10 minutes; repeat if still bleeding.
- Breathe through the mouth, sit forward, spit blood into a bowl. Place an ice pack on the dorsum of the nose or suck on ice.
- If still bleeding, cauterise with silver nitrate:
  - Use a headlight and speculum; remove clots with suction.
  - Apply a cotton ball soaked in 1:200,000 adrenaline for 2 minutes, or use a local anaesthetic spray.
  - Find bleeding points ("small volcanoes"), apply cautery for 2 seconds at a time.
  - Cauterise the base of the vessel before hitting the active bleeding point.
- If the bleeding point cannot be identified, refer to ENT — may try exploration under anaesthesia, arterial ligation, or embolisation.
- If bleeding continues: anterior nasal pack — advance into the nose horizontally and parallel to the hard palate; remove after 24h.
- If bleeding still continues, pack the other nostril ± pressure.
- If still bleeding, use a postnasal pack or Foley urinary catheter.

### Post-bleeding advice

- Don't pick or blow the nose. If you sneeze, do so through the mouth.
- Avoid bending, lifting, or straining. No hot food or drink.
- If bleeding restarts, apply ice to the bridge of the nose and hold the soft lower part continuously for 20 minutes.

## Nasopharyngeal cancer

> [!note] Expanded — previously only a bare epidemiology line with no risk factors beyond geography, despite EBV being a well-established and frequently-tested association. Verified against AAFP's clinical review and current epidemiological literature, Aug 2026.

- **E:** rare in most populations, but a leading cancer in specific well-defined populations — natives of southern China (particularly Hong Kong and Guangdong province), Southeast Asia, the Arctic, and the Middle East/North Africa; risk remains elevated in these populations even after emigration, though at an intermediate level, suggesting a genuine gene-environment interaction rather than either factor alone.
- **R:** **Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)** — a well-established and strongly-associated risk factor, with elevated EBV antibody titres detectable years before diagnosis and used as an actual NPC screening biomarker in endemic areas; consumption of salt-preserved fish (a traditional dietary item in NPC-endemic regions, containing nitrosamines with carcinogenic potential and capable of reactivating EBV, though its independent contribution relative to EBV/genetics may be more modest than historically assumed); family history of NPC; certain HLA class I genotypes; smoking.
- **S/Smx:** neck lump (90% — the most common presenting feature, via metastatic spread to a cervical lymph node); nasal symptoms (bleeding, obstruction, discharge); hearing loss (usually unilateral); unilateral serous otitis media; cranial nerve palsies due to base of skull extension.
- **Dx/Ix:** endoscopy + biopsy; staging by MRI.
- **Mx:** radiotherapy + chemotherapy + surgery.
- **P:** >80% 5-year survival for stage I; <30% for advanced tumours.
