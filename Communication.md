# Communication / Counselling / Procedural Stations (Template C — task-led)

> [!info] Verified — the Advance Care Directives Act 2013 (SA) is confirmed current and in force, Aug 2026 (the SA Law Handbook shows updates as recent as July 2026, including a new provision on substitute decision-maker authority effective 9 July 2026), so the SA-specific legal/consent framework referenced below is accurate and appropriately targeted — this is genuinely the correct jurisdiction-specific reference given a Flinders University (Adelaide, SA) context, not a generic placeholder needing correction. Worth noting for completeness: SA replaced the older Guardianship and Administration Act 1993 medical-agent/enduring-guardian system with the current substitute-decision-maker model under the 2013 Act — any pre-2014 medical power of attorney or enduring guardianship remains valid under transitional provisions, but new arrangements now go through the 2013 Act framework exclusively. The general SPIKES framework and end-of-life communication principles below are not SA-specific and apply nationally/internationally.

## Consent for Blood Transfusion

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Consent for blood transfusion," High yield) — a genuinely distinct consent/counselling station, absent despite the disease-level detail already built in [[10_08_Haemonc_-_Blood_Products_and_Transfusion]] (blood products, indications, complications). This entry covers the consent conversation itself; cross-references the disease-level content for clinical detail rather than repeating it.

**Station framework:** standard informed consent principles apply (indication, benefits, risks, alternatives, and the opportunity to ask questions/decline), but this conversation has some genuinely distinctive content given the specific risks and cultural/religious considerations involved.

**Opening approach:**
- Explain why a transfusion is being recommended in plain language, tied to the patient's actual clinical situation (e.g. "your blood count is low enough that it's affecting how you feel, and a transfusion should help").
- Establish the patient's baseline understanding and any prior transfusion experience or concerns before launching into a standard explanation.

**Key content/checkpoints to cover (checklist):**
- [ ] **What the product is and where it comes from** — donated blood, screened and processed per Australian Red Cross Lifeblood standards; explain the specific product being given (packed red cells, platelets, FFP — see [[10_08_Haemonc_-_Blood_Products_and_Transfusion]] Blood products for the disease-level detail on each, not repeated here) if relevant to the patient's understanding.
- [ ] **Benefits** — specific to the indication (e.g. symptom relief from anaemia, correction of active bleeding/coagulopathy).
- [ ] **Risks** — in proportionate, honest terms rather than either minimising or catastrophising: common/minor reactions (febrile non-haemolytic reactions, minor allergic reactions — see [[10_08_Haemonc_-_Blood_Products_and_Transfusion]] Blood product transfusion complications for the full list and relative frequencies, not repeated here) are relatively common and manageable; serious reactions (haemolytic reactions, TRALI, TACO, anaphylaxis) are rare but should still be mentioned given the consent needs to be genuinely informed; infection transmission risk is now extremely low given current screening standards, worth stating explicitly given this is a common patient concern even though it's a comparatively minor modern risk.
- [ ] **Alternatives** — explain what the alternatives actually are (watchful waiting if clinically appropriate, iron/erythropoiesis-stimulating agents for some anaemias, cell salvage in surgical contexts) and why transfusion is being recommended over them in this specific situation, rather than presenting transfusion as the only option without explanation.
- [ ] **Process** — what the patient can expect practically (monitoring during the transfusion, approximate duration, what to report if they feel unwell during the infusion).
- [ ] **The right to decline** — patients have the right to refuse blood products, and this must be explored respectfully and without pressure, particularly relevant for patients with religious objections (see the Jehovah's Witness point below) — refusal doesn't end the conversation, but shifts it to discussing alternatives and the implications of declining.

**Jehovah's Witness patients — a genuinely important, specific scenario:**
- Many Jehovah's Witnesses decline blood transfusion on religious grounds, though the specific position can vary between individuals — **never assume a blanket refusal without asking the individual patient directly**, since some accept certain fractionated products (e.g. albumin, clotting factor concentrates) while declining whole blood/red cells/plasma/platelets — this is genuinely individual and must be explored rather than assumed from the patient's stated faith alone.
- Cell saver devices (see [[10_08_Haemonc_-_Blood_Products_and_Transfusion]] Cell saver devices) may be an acceptable alternative to some Jehovah's Witness patients specifically, given blood remains connected to the patient's own circulation throughout — worth raising as an option where clinically relevant.
- A capacitous adult's informed refusal of blood products must be respected, even where the clinical team disagrees with the decision or where it carries serious risk — this is a genuine test of respecting patient autonomy, not a scenario to be talked around; document the discussion and decision clearly.
- For a child of Jehovah's Witness parents needing a transfusion, this becomes a different and more complex situation (parental refusal on behalf of a minor is not equivalent to informed adult self-determination) — genuinely difficult and involves urgent senior/legal input rather than a simple parallel to the adult scenario; not detailed further here given the complexity, but worth flagging as a materially different situation if it comes up.

**Useful phrases/scripts:**
- Introducing risk without alarming: "Transfusions are generally very safe, but like any medical treatment there are some risks I want to make sure you understand."
- Exploring religious/personal objection respectfully: "Some people have personal or religious reasons for not wanting blood products — is that something that's relevant for you?" (open, non-assuming phrasing)
- Confirming genuine understanding, not just passive agreement: "Can you tell me in your own words what we've talked about, so I can check I've explained it clearly?"

**Closing:** confirm the patient's decision (consent or informed refusal), document the discussion, arrange the transfusion (or discuss alternatives if declined), safety-net for any symptoms during/after the transfusion that should prompt them to alert staff.

**Common pitfalls / what OSCE mark schemes typically penalise:**
- Presenting transfusion as the only option without genuinely discussing alternatives
- Minimising or omitting risks to make the conversation easier, undermining genuinely informed consent
- Assuming a Jehovah's Witness patient's position on blood products rather than asking directly, or assuming it's an all-or-nothing refusal
- Not exploring the patient's own concerns/questions before moving on
- Failing to check genuine understanding, mistaking passive nodding for informed consent

## Breaking Bad News (New Diagnosis)

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Breaking bad news," General Practice/Preventive Med/Ethics & Communication category, High yield) — a genuinely high-yield, previously-absent station despite extensive disease-level content now built that this framework directly applies to (e.g. a new melanoma diagnosis — see [[09_02_Dermatology_-_Melanocytic_Lesions_and_Mimickers]] Melanoma; a new HIV diagnosis — see [[08_05-06_Infectious_Disease_-_Viral_Infections]] Human Immunodeficiency Virus). This entry covers the general breaking-bad-news framework and two worked applications; cross-references the disease sections for clinical detail rather than repeating it.

**Station framework: SPIKES** (Setting, Perception, Invitation, Knowledge, Empathy, Summarise/Strategy) — the standard, widely-taught framework for this station type, also referenced in the DNACPR entry above; the same underlying skeleton applies regardless of the specific diagnosis being delivered.

**Opening approach:**
- **Setting:** private room, adequate time, minimise interruptions (phone off/silent), sit at the same level as the patient, involve a support person if the patient wishes, have tissues available.
- **Perception:** ask what the patient already knows or suspects before delivering information ("What's your understanding of why we did this test?") — this avoids either over-explaining what they already know or under-preparing them for news they don't expect.
- **Invitation:** gauge how much detail the patient wants and at what pace ("Would it be alright if I go through the results with you now?") — respects patient autonomy over the pace of disclosure, and some patients genuinely want less detail than a default full explanation would give.

**Delivering the news:**
- **Knowledge:** give a brief warning shot ("I'm afraid I have some difficult news") before the diagnosis itself, given an abrupt delivery with no lead-in is jarring; deliver the core information in **plain language, in a single short sentence**, then pause — avoid a long lead-up of technical detail before the actual diagnosis, and avoid euphemism vague enough to be misunderstood.
- **Empathy:** after delivering the news, **stop talking** — allow silence for the reaction, then explicitly name and acknowledge the emotion observed ("I can see this has come as a shock") rather than rushing to fill the silence with further medical information the patient is unlikely to absorb in this moment.
- **Summarise/Strategy:** once the patient is ready (not immediately), summarise the diagnosis, check understanding, and outline next steps and support available — avoid overloading with detailed management planning in the same sitting if the patient is still processing the initial shock; it's appropriate to offer a follow-up conversation for detailed planning.

**Useful phrases/scripts:**
- Warning shot: "I'm afraid the results show something more serious than we'd hoped."
- Checking understanding without being patronising: "I've given you a lot of information — can you tell me what you've taken from what I've said, so I can fill in any gaps?"
- Handling "how long do I have"-type questions: acknowledge the question is natural, be honest about uncertainty where it genuinely exists, avoid false precision.
- Closing on a note of support rather than abandonment: "You're not going through this alone — here's what happens next, and here's how to reach us."

**Worked application — new melanoma diagnosis:** after biopsy confirms melanoma (see [[09_02_Dermatology_-_Melanocytic_Lesions_and_Mimickers]] Melanoma for the disease-level detail), the same SPIKES structure applies: establish what the patient understood about "having a mole checked," deliver the diagnosis plainly, allow the emotional reaction, then explain that **Breslow thickness will determine the next steps** (excision margin, need for further staging) without necessarily quoting exact prognostic percentages in the same sitting unless the patient specifically asks — offering to discuss prognosis in more detail once initial results (thickness, staging) are available is often more appropriate than reciting 5-year survival figures at the point of initial diagnosis.

**Worked application — new HIV diagnosis:** a distinct communication challenge given the historical stigma attached to this diagnosis specifically (see [[08_05-06_Infectious_Disease_-_Viral_Infections]] Human Immunodeficiency Virus for the disease-level detail) — the SPIKES structure still applies, but with particular attention to: **actively countering outdated assumptions** the patient may hold about HIV being a uniformly fatal or rapidly progressive diagnosis, given modern ART means a near-normal life expectancy is achievable with prompt treatment and adherence — this reassurance is clinically accurate and appropriately part of the "knowledge" delivery, not just an empathetic add-on; **confidentiality** is a particularly heightened concern for this diagnosis and worth proactively addressing; and **partner notification/contact tracing** will need to be raised, ideally not in the same breath as the initial diagnosis itself, but flagged as something to discuss once the immediate shock has settled.

**Closing:** summarise what has been discussed, confirm the patient has a clear next step and knows how to make contact with questions, offer written information/support resources where appropriate, arrange concrete follow-up rather than an open-ended "let us know if you need anything."

**Common pitfalls / what OSCE mark schemes typically penalise:**
- Delivering the diagnosis buried in a long technical preamble, or via euphemism vague enough to be misunderstood
- Not pausing/allowing silence after delivering the news, and instead continuing to talk through the patient's emotional reaction
- Overloading with detailed management information before the patient has processed the diagnosis itself
- Failing to check the patient's prior understanding before explaining (Perception step skipped)
- For stigmatised diagnoses specifically (HIV, and to a lesser extent some STIs): unconsciously conveying judgment through tone, word choice, or excessive/defensive reassurance that can itself feel patronising
- Not offering a clear next step or follow-up plan before closing

## Discussing "Do Not Attempt Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation" (DNACPR / Not-for-Resuscitation)

**Station framework:** no single universal mnemonic, but SPIKES (Setting, Perception, Invitation, Knowledge, Empathy, Summarise) is commonly adapted for this conversation, alongside general shared decision-making principles.

**Opening approach:**
- Ensure privacy, adequate time, minimise interruptions; involve family/substitute decision-maker if the patient wishes.
- Assess the patient's (or substitute decision-maker's) current understanding of their condition and prognosis before introducing the topic ("What have the other doctors told you about how things are going?").
- Explain the purpose of the conversation clearly and gently — this is about future care planning, not abandoning current treatment.

**Key content/checkpoints to cover (checklist):**
- [ ] Explain what CPR actually involves (chest compressions, defibrillation, intubation) — many patients have inaccurate expectations, often from media portrayal
- [ ] Explain realistic outcomes of CPR in this specific clinical context (survival rates in the frail/multimorbid/terminally unwell are much lower than commonly assumed) — be honest but compassionate
- [ ] Clarify that a DNACPR decision applies specifically to CPR and does **not** mean withdrawal of other active treatment (pain relief, antibiotics, other supportive care continue as clinically appropriate) — this is the most common misconception and needs explicit correction
- [ ] Explore the patient's own values, goals of care, and what quality of life means to them
- [ ] Establish who has decision-making authority if the patient lacks capacity (substitute decision-maker per SA's Advance Care Directives Act 2013 framework — confirm the applicable state legislation if outside SA) — see [[Clinical-Process-EBM-Consent-Capacity]] for full capacity/substitute-decision-maker detail
- [ ] Document the outcome clearly and ensure it is communicated to the wider care team

**Useful phrases/scripts:**
- Naming emotion: "I can see this is difficult to hear/talk about."
- Checking understanding: "Can you tell me in your own words what we've just discussed?"
- Handling distress/anger: pause, acknowledge the emotion explicitly, don't rush to fill silence, avoid becoming defensive.
- Reframing away from "giving up": "This isn't about doing less for you — it's about making sure the care you receive matches what matters most to you."

**Closing:** summarise the decision reached (or that more time/a further conversation is needed — this is a legitimate outcome, not a failure), safety-net (explain the decision can be revisited), arrange follow-up, offer written information if available.

**Common pitfalls / what OSCE mark schemes typically penalise:**
- Leading with medical jargon ("code status," "CPR") before establishing the patient's understanding
- Implying CPR is being "withheld" rather than framing it as a clinical judgement about likely benefit vs harm
- Not exploring the patient's own values/goals before presenting a recommendation
- Rushing the conversation or failing to pause for the patient's emotional reaction
- Conflating DNACPR with broader treatment withdrawal
- Failing to clarify decision-making authority when the patient lacks capacity

## Management of Patients with Multiple Chronic Medical Problems

> [!note] CSV listed conditions for this task: COPD/asthma, CKD, congestive heart failure, ischaemic heart disease, diabetes, cirrhosis, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoporosis, HIV, stroke, depression — this is a task/approach-led row (multimorbidity management as a skill), not a single disease, hence its place here rather than in a system file.

**Station framework:** no single named mnemonic; the core skill is a structured, patient-centred approach to competing priorities rather than a disease-by-disease checklist.

**Opening approach:**
- Establish the patient's own priorities first — with multiple conditions, "textbook optimal" management of each individually is often neither feasible nor what the patient wants (competing treatment burdens, polypharmacy risk, appointment burden).
- Ask what matters most to them functionally (independence, symptom control, avoiding hospital, specific activities) rather than starting from a disease-first frame.

**Key content/checkpoints to cover (checklist):**
- [ ] Medication reconciliation — full current medication list, checking for interactions, duplications, and drugs that worsen one condition while treating another (e.g. NSAIDs worsening CKD/HF; β-blockers needing caution in reactive airway disease)
- [ ] Identify which conditions are actively destabilising vs stable/well-controlled — prioritise the former
- [ ] Screen for the cumulative effect of multimorbidity itself: polypharmacy burden, functional decline, falls risk, cognitive load of self-management, carer burden
- [ ] Screen for depression/anxiety specifically — very common and easily overlooked in the context of complex physical multimorbidity, and can significantly worsen engagement with all other conditions
- [ ] Coordinate rather than duplicate — identify a lead clinician/GP as care coordinator, avoid conflicting advice from multiple specialists
- [ ] Discuss realistic, negotiated goals of care rather than defaulting to maximal guideline-directed therapy for every condition in isolation
- [ ] Plan follow-up that consolidates review where possible (shared GP management plan, care coordination) rather than adding further isolated appointments

**Useful phrases/scripts:**
- "With everything going on, what's the one thing that would make the biggest difference to how you're feeling day to day?"
- "Some of your medications can work against each other — let's go through what you're actually taking and why."

**Closing:** summarise the agreed priorities and plan, confirm the patient's understanding, arrange coordinated follow-up, safety-net for any red-flag deterioration relevant to their most active condition(s).

**Common pitfalls / what OSCE mark schemes typically penalise:**
- Working through each condition as an isolated checklist without addressing interactions between them
- Not eliciting patient priorities before proposing a management plan
- Missing depression screening in a complex physical-multimorbidity context
- Failing to address polypharmacy/medication burden explicitly
- Not identifying a care-coordination plan (who is the lead clinician)

## Caring for the Elderly in the Community (Dementia, Mobility, Parkinson's, Recurrent Falls)

> [!note] CSV row for this task/approach-led station spans multiple already-built disease entities — dementia (see [[04_Neurology]] Dementias), Parkinson's disease (see [[04_Neurology]] Movement Disorders), and falls/mobility overlaps with delirium and gait disorders also in [[04_Neurology]]. This entry covers the holistic community-care approach itself, not the individual disease-level content, which is cross-referenced rather than repeated here.

**Station framework:** no single named mnemonic; the core skill is a structured functional/holistic assessment that integrates multiple chronic conditions with the practical realities of living independently (or semi-independently) in the community — similar in spirit to the multimorbidity approach above, but with a specific focus on functional safety and the family/carer network.

**Opening approach:**
- Establish who else is involved in the person's care and daily life (family, carers, community services) — much of this assessment depends on collateral information, since the patient (especially with cognitive impairment) may under-report difficulties.
- Ask directly about the person's own goals: staying at home vs accepting more support, independence vs safety trade-offs — these are genuine, often difficult, value judgments the person (and family) need to make, not something to be decided for them.

**Key content/checkpoints to cover (checklist):**
- [ ] **Falls history** — frequency, circumstances (mechanical trip vs syncope/pre-syncope vs cognitive/gait-related), injuries sustained, fear of falling (which itself causes deconditioning and further fall risk); screen for reversible contributors — postural hypotension, polypharmacy (sedatives, antihypertensives), vision/hearing impairment, home hazards, footwear
- [ ] **Cognitive screen** — even if not the presenting concern, cognitive impairment changes safety calculus for every other domain (medication adherence, falls risk, financial/legal capacity) — see [[04_Neurology]] Dementias for the disease-level work-up
- [ ] **Mobility/functional assessment** — activities of daily living (ADLs: washing, dressing, toileting, feeding) and instrumental ADLs (IADLs: shopping, cooking, finances, medications, transport) — the IADL/ADL distinction matters for care-package planning, since IADL difficulty typically precedes ADL difficulty
- [ ] **Medication review** — polypharmacy is common and compounds falls/cognitive risk; specifically review for sedating, anticholinergic, and hypotension-causing medications
- [ ] **Home environment and safety** — stairs, rugs/trip hazards, bathroom safety (grab rails, shower access), lighting, smoke alarms, ability to summon help if needed (personal alarm)
- [ ] **Carer assessment** — carer wellbeing and burnout risk is a legitimate and often-overlooked part of this assessment; a care arrangement that isn't sustainable for the carer isn't actually sustainable for the patient either
- [ ] **Nutrition** — screen for unintentional weight loss, difficulty shopping/cooking, social isolation affecting appetite
- [ ] **Advance care planning** — goals of care, substitute decision-maker, and end-of-life wishes discussions, ideally initiated while the person retains capacity rather than deferred until a crisis

**Useful phrases/scripts:**
- "Tell me about a typical day — what do you do from when you wake up to when you go to bed?" (a functional, non-judgmental way to surface ADL/IADL difficulties the person might not otherwise volunteer)
- "How are you finding things, looking after [person]?" (directed at the carer, acknowledging their role explicitly)
- "What matters most to you about how things go from here?" (opening an advance care planning conversation without being blunt about prognosis)

**Closing:** summarise the identified risks and agreed plan, confirm both the patient's and carer's understanding and agreement, arrange appropriate referrals (e.g. geriatrician, falls clinic, occupational therapy home assessment, community nursing, aged care assessment team), safety-net for acute deterioration and who to contact.

**Common pitfalls / what OSCE mark schemes typically penalise:**
- Focusing only on the presenting condition (e.g. the fall) without the broader functional/cognitive/social context
- Not involving or assessing the carer
- Missing a medication review despite polypharmacy being a major modifiable falls/cognition risk factor
- Failing to ask about the person's own goals and preferences, defaulting instead to a purely risk-minimisation approach
- Not considering advance care planning until a crisis forces the conversation
