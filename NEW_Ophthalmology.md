## Acute Visual Loss

- **D:** loss of vision developing over seconds to days. The first three questions do almost all the diagnostic work: **is it painful or painless? one eye or both? and is the loss transient or persistent?**
- **DDx:**
  - **Sudden, painless, monocular — the classic emergency group:**
    - **Central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO)** — sudden profound loss, a pale retina with a **cherry-red spot** at the macula, and a **relative afferent pupillary defect**. Treated as a stroke of the eye: it requires urgent ophthalmology involvement *and* urgent stroke work-up, because it carries the same embolic risk as a cerebral TIA.
    - **Giant cell arteritis with arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy** — **the diagnosis that must not be missed**, because untreated it blinds the other eye. Suspect in anyone over 50 with visual loss plus headache, scalp tenderness, **jaw claudication** (the most specific symptom), temporal artery tenderness, polymyalgic symptoms, fever or weight loss.
    - **Central retinal vein occlusion** — less abrupt, "blood and thunder" fundus with widespread haemorrhages.
    - **Retinal detachment** — preceded by **flashes and floaters**, then a curtain or shadow advancing across the field.
    - **Vitreous haemorrhage** — sudden floaters and haze, commonly in proliferative diabetic retinopathy.
    - **Non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy**; **occipital (cortical) stroke** — which causes a **homonymous field defect** and is often described by the patient as loss in one eye, so **always test the fields**.
  - **Sudden, painful:**
    - **Acute angle-closure glaucoma** — painful red eye, haloes around lights, nausea and vomiting, a hazy cornea and a fixed mid-dilated pupil; the eye feels hard.
    - **Optic neuritis** — pain on eye movement, reduced colour vision (**red desaturation**), a relative afferent pupillary defect, typically in a younger patient; associated with multiple sclerosis.
    - **Keratitis or corneal ulcer** (especially in contact lens wearers), **anterior uveitis**, **endophthalmitis** after intraocular surgery or injection, **chemical injury** or trauma.
  - **Transient:** **amaurosis fugax** (embolic — a TIA of the eye requiring the same urgency and work-up as any TIA), migraine with aura, papilloedema with transient visual obscurations, hypoperfusion.
  - **Gradual but noticed suddenly:** cataract, macular degeneration, chronic glaucoma, diabetic maculopathy, compressive lesion — commonly "discovered" when the good eye is covered.
- **Focused Hx:** **exact time and speed of onset**; **one eye or both — and whether the patient has actually covered each eye to check**; painful or painless; **pain on eye movement**; **flashes, floaters, or a curtain/shadow**; haloes around lights; **transient episodes preceding this one**; **headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, shoulder and hip girdle stiffness, fever, weight loss** — the GCA screen, asked in every patient over 50; **contact lens wear**; trauma or chemical exposure; recent eye surgery or intravitreal injection; **diabetes and its control, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, smoking, atrial fibrillation, previous stroke or TIA, known vasculitis**; medications including anticoagulants and **amiodarone, ethambutol and hydroxychloroquine**; neurological symptoms; and previous ophthalmic history.
- **Examination:**
  - **Visual acuity in each eye separately, with the patient's glasses or through a pinhole** — this is the vital sign of the eye and must be recorded, not estimated.
  - **Pupils, specifically testing for a relative afferent pupillary defect with the swinging-flashlight test** — an RAPD indicates optic nerve or extensive retinal disease and is the single most informative bedside sign here.
  - **Visual fields to confrontation in each eye** — to identify a homonymous defect indicating a retrochiasmal (cerebral) cause.
  - **Colour vision** (red desaturation) where optic neuritis is suspected.
  - **Eye movements** and whether they provoke pain.
  - **External examination** — redness and its pattern (ciliary flush), corneal clarity, discharge, proptosis, lid position.
  - **Fundoscopy**, dilated where safe and appropriate — **but do not dilate if angle-closure glaucoma is suspected**.
  - **Palpate the globe** for hardness and **palpate the temporal arteries** for tenderness, thickening or absent pulsation.
  - **Cardiovascular examination** — pulse for atrial fibrillation, blood pressure, carotid bruits.
  - **Fluorescein staining** where a corneal cause is possible; **intraocular pressure measurement** where glaucoma is suspected.
- **First-line Ix:**
  - **ESR and CRP urgently, plus FBC and platelets**, in any patient over 50 with visual loss — **the GCA screen. Inflammatory markers can be normal in biopsy-proven GCA, so a normal result does not exclude it**, and treatment should not be withheld on that basis alone.
  - **Glucose/HbA1c, lipids, UEC, coagulation profile**; **ECG** for atrial fibrillation.
  - **Temporal artery ultrasound and/or temporal artery biopsy** where GCA is suspected — arranged urgently, **but never allowed to delay treatment**.
  - **Urgent CT or MRI brain** where a cortical cause, a compressive lesion or optic neuritis is suspected; **MRI with contrast** for optic neuritis and demyelination.
  - **Carotid duplex and echocardiography** for embolic causes, as for a TIA.
  - **Intraocular pressure**; **B-scan ultrasound** where the fundal view is obscured by vitreous haemorrhage.
  - **Same-day ophthalmology review** is itself the key "investigation" in most of this differential — formal slit-lamp examination, dilated fundoscopy and optical coherence tomography are not intern-level procedures, and the correct action is referral with an accurate acuity, pupil and field assessment already documented.

> [!danger] **Two things that cause avoidable permanent blindness in this presentation.** First, **not asking about jaw claudication and scalp tenderness in an older patient**, and not starting treatment while awaiting biopsy — in suspected GCA, **high-dose corticosteroid is started immediately on clinical suspicion, before biopsy, because the fellow eye is at risk within days**. *(The specific corticosteroid dose and route are deliberately omitted — they differ between guidelines and the Australian sources are egress-blocked. Escalate immediately; do not delay the discussion while looking up a dose.)* Second, **failing to check visual acuity and pupils** and referring an eye without them, which removes the information the ophthalmologist needs to triage.
